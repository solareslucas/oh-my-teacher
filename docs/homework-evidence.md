# Evidence behind homework design

This document justifies the **Practice/homework** rules in `.omp/skills/adaptive-tutor/SKILL.md` and the subject profiles in `.omp/skills/adaptive-tutor/references/subjects.md`. It records what was actually opened, with what access, and what remains inference.

## Scope and method

- **Questions:** (1) Which kinds of homework tasks support learning, including whether to use investigations? (2) What delivery format should homework use? (3) Which subjects need their own submission and assessment systems?
- **Type:** bounded narrative scan on 2026-10-24, not a systematic review. No exhaustiveness claim; no formal risk-of-bias appraisal was applied.
- **Execution:** question 1–2 sources were opened by the maintaining agent. Question 3 sources were found and opened by a delegated agent; the maintaining agent re-opened nine of those passages. Entries marked † were not independently re-verified by the maintaining agent.
- **Applicability:** most studies involve school or university students, mainly in mathematics and science. Applying them to a one-to-one AI tutor and to adult learners is an extrapolation. This configuration's learning effect has not been evaluated.

## Claims and how they shape the rules

| Claim | Sources | Rule it supports |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C1. Practice testing (retrieval) is rated high utility; closed-book quizzes have strong evidence; classroom quizzing has a medium overall effect (g ≈ 0.50), moderated by format consistency and corrective feedback. | S1 §8.5; S2 rec. 5b; S3 abstract | Section A; first attempt without notes; format matches the target assessment |
| C2. Spacing is rated high utility; re-exposure weeks later is recommended (moderate evidence). | S1 §9; S2 rec. 1 | Earlier objectives keep returning in A |
| C3. Interleaving is rated moderate utility; in a cluster RCT with 54 seventh-grade classes, interleaved practice beat blocked practice on a test one month later (61% vs 38%, d = 0.83). | S1 §10.5; S5 | Section C; evidence is mostly mathematics |
| C4. Alternating worked examples with problems has moderate evidence; examples help novices but lose value as expertise grows, and gradual fading helps; the best strategy depends on the learning goal. S6 frames expertise as experience "in a specific domain" and reports that adaptive fading by current skill level outperformed fixed fading or problem solving in one lab and (delayed test only) one classroom study. | S2 rec. 2; S6; S7 | Section B with fading; composition by evidence state, not fixed counts; support per component |
| C5. Deep explanatory questions have strong evidence; prompting self-explanation has g = .55. | S2 rec. 7; S8 | Section D; submit reasoning |
| C6. Feedback has a medium effect (d = 0.48 without extreme values) with large heterogeneity; more information means more effect (reinforcement only, d = 0.24). Online homework with immediate feedback raised scores (g = 0.18), more for lower prior achievers; the package also included teacher training. | S9; S10 | Feedback must explain what failed and what to do next |
| C7. Rubrics have a positive, moderate effect on academic performance. | S11 | Rubric before the attempt; tutor evaluates against it |
| C8. Guidance is pivotal in inquiry learning (72 studies, science and mathematics, ages 5–22). S12 reports that a prior meta-analysis (Alfieri et al. 2011, not opened) found minimal-guidance inquiry less effective than explicit instruction and guided inquiry more effective. | S12 | Section E: investigations are included, always guided |
| C9. Rereading, highlighting, and summarization are rated low utility. | S1 §§3.5, 4.5, 7.5 | Tutor summaries are reference material, not homework |
| C10. In a field experiment with nearly 1,000 high-school students, unrestricted GPT-4 access raised practice grades but lowered later unaided grades by 17%; a tutor with safeguards (hints instead of answers) largely mitigated the harm. | S13 | First attempt without AI; declared help; no answer keys in assignments |
| C11. Readers scored typed compositions lower than identical handwritten ones (five studies, grades 4–10). Teacher written comments are under-studied. | S14 | Medium chosen for inspectability and target match, not appearance; feedback on drafts |
| C12. Self-assessment interventions show a meaningful mean effect on academic performance (g = 0.585; meta-analysis of 175 self- and peer-assessment studies), larger in repeated-measures than in experimental or quasi-experimental designs. Implementations vary widely (20 categories), and a prior meta-analysis found a smaller mean effect for simple self-grading (0.34, with 12 of 44 effect sizes negative; reported in S15, original not opened). S16 reports effects on self-regulated learning of 0.23–0.65 and on self-efficacy of 0.73 (19 studies). | S15; S16 | A tutor-written "before you submit" check, not self-grading; its usefulness for each learner is judged by points lost to omissions |

## Inferences, not findings

- That a homework section structure (subject sections A–E, with F reserved for tutor feedback) combining these practices works as a whole has not been tested; each component has its own evidence. Tutor-owned grading and evaluation justification, brief natural-language help disclosure rather than a table, and no mandatory learner self-assessment, doubt, or closing report are pedagogical design choices, not source findings.
- The "before you submit" check (two fixed questions, one task-specific, one minute, nothing written or graded) is a design inference from C12, not a tested intervention. No opened source evaluates this format. The earlier mapping of C7 to learner self-assessment in F was also an inference: S11's only significant moderator was that combined self- and peer assessment beat self-assessment alone, from five effect sizes. Tracking each learner's omission points is a practical check, not a controlled comparison.
- No source found that the delivery medium itself (Typst, chat, photo) changes learning. Medium choice rests on inspectability and on C1's format-consistency moderator.
- Composition rules (more B for novices, more C–E with independence) apply C4 to other sections by analogy.
- Calibrating support per component of a single task (worked examples for a new language's tools, problem solving for design skills already shown), and probing carried-over habits from an adjacent domain with predict-then-explain items, extend S6 to a finer grain. S6 was re-opened on 2026-10-25 (introduction text); no opened source tests component-level calibration or negative transfer between domains directly.
- The rule that an example must not share the task's solution structure (renaming its parts must not yield the answer), and that success on a structural twin counts as supported, is a design inference from C4 (examples help novices; independence must be shown without them) and C10 (practice success with the answer available did not carry over to unaided work). C10 concerns unrestricted AI answers, not worked examples; no opened source tests example–task structural overlap directly. Added 2026-10-26 after a learner reported an exercise that could be solved by renaming the tutor's example.
- S4's figures (multiple-choice practice tests g = 0.70 vs short answer g = 0.48) are known only via S3 and conflict with S2's preference for recall formats; the rules therefore follow format consistency with the target assessment rather than a fixed item type.

## What would change these rules

Direct evaluations of AI-tutor homework designs with delayed, unaided outcomes; evidence for adult self-directed learners; and subject-specific trials outside mathematics and science.
